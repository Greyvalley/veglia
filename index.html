The case

One night in October

An illustrative case, built from real conditions.

Early October, a high pass in the Dolomites. Anna, 34. By 3 the cloud is down; by 4, early snow has buried the trail markings.

Her phone is at 6%, no signal. The last hut is two hours back, and full. The bivouac is on a ridge she can no longer see.

She does what most people do. She keeps walking, and hopes.

This is not rare.

13,037mountain rescue missions in Italy in 2025: the highest number ever recorded, up 8% in one year.1,2
52.1%of those rescued are hikers, the largest group. Many are lost, or cannot get back because of bad weather and poor visibility.1
96%of the people rescued were not Alpine Club members and had no insurance of their own.1
779rescues in the Belluno Dolomites alone, in 2025.4

The solution

The same night, with Veglia.

Three days before, the AI that runs Veglia saw the storm in the forecast and called the keeper. An experienced volunteer climbed up, stocked the emergency boxes, and set the beacons to storm mode.

At 4 o'clock, in the snow, Anna sees a slow amber light pulsing through the white. She walks toward it.

On the pod's wall: a power outlet, unlocked all winter, and a box with a foil blanket, water, a headlamp and food. Her phone charges. She sends one message: I'm safe. In the keeper's pod, someone has watched the trail all afternoon, and walks over.

She never booked. It didn't matter.

What Veglia is

A watch of six on the mountain.

Five hiker pods, each sleeping up to four in bunk beds, and one keeper's pod, all within 500 metres, placed so no pod can see another. Alone with the mountain, never truly alone.

One pod.

Closed, it is a stone. Open, it is a lantern.

A long, low capsule in blackened steel, about 8.8 by 4.6 metres (28 ft 10 in × 15 ft 1 in), rounded like a stone worn by water.

One long glass wall, with folding timber shutters: open in summer, closed in winter.

The solar panels are the skin of the shell. At the bedroom end, an amber beacon.

What's inside

The shell. Its corners are rounded and camouflaged into the rock. The shutters stack to one side in summer and close across the glass in winter, with the entry always left free. The bathroom at one end has fixed frosted glass. The solar panels are set flush into the steel, so the whole pod still reads as one dark stone.

Inside. A bed for one or two at the far end, and two chairs and a small table facing the glass. A small kitchen with a sink, a fridge and a hot plate. A walk-in shower that recycles its own water, and a separate dry area with a separating dry toilet. The water tank hides under the bed, sunk a foot below the floor and reached through a small hatch; the batteries stand in a closet beside the wardrobe.

On the roof: the solar skin, a satellite dish, and the vents. Outside: the amber beacon, an emergency power outlet, and a supply box.

Design it simple. Every part has a reason to be there.

Built in the valley. Flown up. Leaves no trace.

How the warmth works

Nothing to light, nothing to tend.

Shell. In December a pod needs only about 4–8 kWh of heat a day, little enough for the winter sun to supply.

Shutters. The timber shutters open to let the sun in by day, and close at night and whenever the pod is empty, to keep the warmth inside.

Ready. Two small infrared panels, one over the bed and one in the bathroom. The day before you arrive, the AI warms the pod with spare midday sun.

Air. Vents at both ends of the pod bring in fresh air and keep the heat inside. Even the cooking is a hot plate.

Empty. When the pod is empty, a small warm core keeps the water and the batteries from freezing.

How it runs

In summer, a private refuge.

Booked online. Keyless entry: the door opens only for the person who booked.

One night to one week. No more.

A breathing day between guests. The tank refills, the batteries recharge, the pod airs out. The pod is resting is an honest answer.

Checkout with photos. Lights off, breakers off, door locked, each step photographed. You can't check out until the photos are in, and the AI checks them. That is how an empty pod stays clean.

In winter, a lifeline.

The outside outlets stay unlocked all season.

The emergency boxes are stocked at the start of winter and refilled when the AI says they are low.

The beacons pulse, brighter when a storm arrives.

When a storm is coming, a person comes first.

In a storm, one pod can open as an emergency room for anyone caught outside, but only after a person says yes.

The AI keeps the ordinary days.

It takes bookings and manages the breathing days. It opens and locks the doors. It checks checkout photos. It watches the power, the water, the temperature and the security sensors, over satellite. It watches the weather. And it knows when to call a human.

People keep the dangerous ones.

A volunteer keeper lives in the keeper's pod for three months at a time, in spring, summer, and fall. In winter, an experienced volunteer comes up before every storm and keeps watch.

AI and people take turns keeping the mountain safe.

How it begins

First a keeper, then the watch.

YEAR 1

The keeper's pod, alone

Someone lives in it for a full season and learns what drawings cannot: the solar through a week of cloud, the shutters in a freeze, the beacon from the trail. Every mistake is made once, before a single guest arrives.

YEAR 2 ONWARD

The watch grows, one pod at a time

Then the keeper helps choose where the other five pods will stand.

Someone has to know the mountain before strangers can trust it.

Built on things that already work

Nothing in Veglia needs to be invented. Every piece already works somewhere else.

Scooter apps already require a photo before a ride can end.

Keyless cabins in the woods already run with nobody on site.

Satellite internet already reaches mountain huts.

Veglia is the first to put them together for the mountain.
